The Oldest Traces of Faith

A catalogue of the earliest physical evidence ever uncovered for five of the world's great religions — from Bronze Age fire altars to radiocarbon-dated Qur'an folios. Dates are approximate and reflect mainstream scholarship.

2500
BCE

to 100 BCE

Hinduism

From Indus Valley seals to the earliest mention of Vedic gods abroad

  • Fire altars of Kalibangan · c. 2500 BCE — a raised mud-brick altar with a central stele, evidence of a sophisticated ritual fire cult in the Indus Valley Civilization, a possible ancestor of Vedic fire sacrifice.
  • The Pashupati seal · c. 1900 BCE — found at Mohenjo-daro; a horned figure seated in a yogic posture surrounded by animals, widely read as a proto-Shiva figure.
  • The Mitanni treaty · c. 1350 BCE — a Hittite-Mitanni peace treaty that invokes the Vedic gods Mitra, Varuna, Indra, and the Nasatyas as divine witnesses. The earliest written evidence of the Vedic pantheon — found not in India but in Anatolia.
  • Bharhut and Sanchi stupa railings · c. 150–100 BCE — carved gateways and railings depicting Jataka tales and yaksha/yakshi nature deities, among the oldest large-scale visual art of the tradition.
  • The Heliodorus pillar · c. 113 BCE — a Garuda-topped column by a Greek ambassador proclaiming devotion to Vasudeva; the earliest archaeological evidence of the Bhagavata (Vaishnava) cult.
  • Deity evidence — Kanishka's coins · c. 127–150 CE — Kushan gold coinage depicting Shiva (with trident and bull), Buddha, and a pantheon of Iranian, Greek, and Indian gods; among the earliest named images of Hindu deities.

Note: Whether the Indus culture was "Hindu" is actively debated. The Rigveda was composed c. 1500–1200 BCE but preserved orally for millennia before being written down.

Achaemenid to Sasanian

Zoroastrianism

Royal inscriptions, Magi, and fire temples

  • Behistun inscription of Darius I · c. 520 BCE — repeatedly invokes Ahura Mazda and frames history as a battle of truth against the Lie, in language closely matching Zoroastrian theology.
  • Xerxes' "Daiva" inscription at Persepolis · c. 485 BCE — boasts of destroying a sanctuary of the daiva (demons) and worshipping Auramazda "according to Arta (Asha)" — nearly a creed.
  • Naqsh-e Rustam royal tombs · c. 5th–4th c. BCE — Achaemenid kings buried beneath reliefs of the winged Faravahar/Ahura Mazda figure hovering above the king, an icon that endures in the faith today.
  • Accounts of the Magi · 5th c. BCE — Herodotus describes Persian priests, the magoi, who perform sacrifices and interpret dreams; the earliest outside literary testimony to the priestly tradition.
  • Takht-e Soleyman (Adur Gushnasp fire temple) · Sasanian era, 3rd–7th c. CE — one of the best-preserved archaeological fire temples of ancient Iran, built around a sacred lake.
  • Deity evidence — Ardashir I's coinage · c. 224–240 CE — the founder of the Sasanian Empire issued coins showing a fire altar flanked by attendants, the visual heart of later Zoroastrian iconography.

Note: Zarathustra himself may have lived as early as 1500–1000 BCE, but no artifacts from that era survive. The oldest surviving Avesta manuscripts are medieval (c. 13th–14th c. CE).

BCE

to 1st c. CE

Judaism

Stelae, amulets, ostraca, and the greatest manuscript find ever

  • Merneptah Stele · c. 1207 BCE — an Egyptian victory stele bearing the earliest known mention of "Israel" outside the Bible: "Israel is laid waste, its seed is no more."
  • Mesha Stele · c. 840 BCE — a Moabite king boasts of conquering Israelite towns "for Chemosh" and taking vessels of Yahweh; the earliest mention of the Israelite deity outside the Bible.
  • Tel Dan stele · c. 9th–8th c. BCE — an Aramean victory inscription mentioning the "House of David," the earliest reference to David.
  • Kuntillet Ajrud inscriptions · c. 800 BCE — blessings "by Yahweh of Samaria and his asherah" painted beside drawings of a cow and Bes-like figures; a rare window into pre-exilic Israelite folk religion.
  • Deity evidence — Judean pillar figurines · c. 750–586 BCE — thousands of clay female figurines cradling their breasts, found in Judahite homes and widely interpreted as the goddess Asherah, Yahweh's once-consort.
  • Ketef Hinnom silver scroll amulets · 7th–6th c. BCE — tiny silver scrolls from a Jerusalem tomb inscribed with the priestly blessing of Numbers 6:24–26, the oldest physical biblical text ever found.
  • Dead Sea Scrolls · c. 250 BCE – 70 CE — some 900 manuscripts hidden in the Qumran caves, including the earliest known copies of the Hebrew Bible, roughly a thousand years older than any previously surviving biblical manuscript.

Note: Together, these finds trace the evolution from the Yahwistic religion of Iron Age Israel — where goddesses were worshipped in homes — to the aniconic, scriptural Judaism of the Second Temple period.

c. 125
CE

to 4th c. CE

Christianity

From secret scraps of papyrus to basilicas

  • P52 (Rylands fragment) · c. 125 CE — a papyrus fragment of John's Gospel, the oldest known New Testament manuscript.
  • Alexamenos graffito · c. 200 CE — a mocking Roman wall carving of a man worshipping a crucified donkey, the earliest pictorial reference to the crucifixion.
  • Deity evidence — the Good Shepherd, Catacombs of Priscilla · c. 200–250 CE — Christ portrayed as a shepherd carrying a sheep on his shoulders; the earliest known image of Jesus. For centuries, Christians avoided depicting the crucified adult Christ.
  • Megiddo mosaic church inscription · c. 230 CE — a mosaic floor praying to "God Jesus Christ," the earliest known church dedication.
  • Dura-Europos house church · c. 233 CE — a private home converted into a church with a baptistery and the earliest known Christian narrative wall paintings.
  • Aqaba church · c. 293 CE — in modern Jordan, often identified as the earliest purpose-built church building yet excavated.

Note: Because early Christians were a persecuted minority, physical evidence appears surprisingly late — mostly after 200 CE, then exploding after Constantine's Edict of Milan (313 CE).

c. 644
CE

to 8th c. CE

Islam

Qur'an folios, the first inscriptions, and the earliest monuments

  • The Birmingham Qur'an folios · 568–645 CE — radiocarbon-dated parchment possibly written within Muhammad's own lifetime; the oldest known Qur'an manuscript.
  • Zuhayr inscription · 644 CE — a rock carving in Saudi Arabia dated to "year 24" of the hijri era; the earliest known Arabic inscription referencing the era of Muhammad.
  • PERF 558 papyrus · 642 CE — a Greek-Arabic receipt from Egypt, the earliest dated document of the Islamic state.
  • Deity evidence — "standing caliph" coinage · c. 685–705 CE — gold solidi naming "Muhammad rasul Allah" (Muhammad is the messenger of God); since Islam forbids images of God, the divine is conveyed through calligraphy rather than a face.
  • The Sanaa palimpsest · c. 671–705 CE — a Qur'an whose lower erased text differs slightly from the standard text, a unique witness to the scripture's early transmission.
  • Dome of the Rock · 691–692 CE — the earliest surviving Islamic monument; its interior and exterior bands carry the earliest lengthy Islamic texts carved in stone, predating complete Qur'an manuscripts.

Note: Strictly speaking, the "oldest evidence" for Islam sits unusually close to the religion's founding — some manuscripts may even predate the traditional dating of Muhammad's death (632 CE).
